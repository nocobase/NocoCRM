# Company signals

Goal: tell the people selling to a company the few things that happened to it lately that change what they should do. A signal is worth their attention when it makes a purchase more or less likely, changes who decides, or gives them a reason to get in touch. Report nothing rather than something routine.

## 1. What counts

- **Funding**: a funding round, a strategic investment, an IPO filing or listing. New money usually means new projects and budget.
- **Hiring**: hiring at scale, a new office, factory, campus or market. Growth means new teams, new processes and systems to support them.
- **Leadership**: a new or departing executive, above all the people who decide on systems: the CEO, the CIO or CTO, the head of digital transformation, IT or operations. A new decision maker is a reason to introduce ourselves; a departing one may put a deal at risk.
- **Business**: a major product launch, an acquisition or merger, a large contract or tender won, a restructuring, a digital-transformation or domestic-substitution (信创) programme announced. These bring new systems to build or old ones to merge.
- **Risk**: layoffs, a lawsuit or regulatory penalty against the company itself, financial trouble, a project cancelled. These may delay or end a deal.

## 2. What to leave out

- Routine figures: monthly sales or deliveries, quarterly results that hold no surprise, share price moves, rankings and awards.
- Marketing: campaigns, sponsorships, speeches, conference appearances, executives' interviews.
- Events of another company with a similar name, of a parent, sister or subsidiary company named differently, or of a brand the company only sells. When the pages do not make clear it is this company (its full name, its city, its website), leave the event out.
- Anything older than the period asked about, or undated.

## 3. How to tell it

- One sentence for what happened, in plain words, with the figures the pages give (an amount, a headcount, a name).
- At most two sentences on why it matters to selling to this company, only when that is not obvious.
- The day the event happened, not the day the article was published.
- The pages that state it, cited by their numbers. Never an event the pages do not state, and never a link of your own.
